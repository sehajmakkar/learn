## The problem: too much at once

A celebrity posts a Snip link to 50 million followers, and redirects jump to three times Snip's usual peak. Snip can
add servers, but that takes minutes, and the spike is now.

Without a plan, here's what happens. Requests arrive faster than servers finish them, so they <Term t="message-queue">queue</Term>. Each request
waits longer, until it's waiting longer than the client's timeout. The client gives up and retries, adding more load.
The server eventually reaches each abandoned request and does the full work anyway, for nobody. It's **busy at
100%**, and almost **none of its work is useful**.

That's the key distinction:

- **Throughput** is how many requests a second the server handles.
- **Goodput** is how many it handles **in time for the client to use the answer**.

Past capacity, throughput can stay high while goodput collapses towards zero. The goal of this lesson is to keep
goodput at capacity even when demand is far beyond it.

## Backpressure: bounded queues

Every queue in a system should have a **limit**. When it's full, the producer is told so, and slows down or fails
fast, instead of the queue growing without end. That push upstream is **backpressure**.

- A server's request queue that's full answers new requests with `503` straight away.
- A job queue that's full makes producers wait, or reject new jobs, instead of holding hours of backlog.
- A client that sees backpressure (a `503` or `429`, maybe with `Retry-After`) backs off (<L id="03.09" />).

Unbounded queues feel safe ("we never drop anything!"), but all they do is turn an overload into very high latency
and, eventually, running out of memory.

## Load shedding: say no early and cheaply

**Load shedding** is a server deciding, as each request arrives, whether it has room to serve it well. If not, it
rejects it immediately with a cheap error, before doing any real work. A rejected request costs microseconds; an
accepted one that times out costs the full work and helps nobody.

Common signals for "no room": too many requests in progress, a queue past its limit, CPU above a threshold, or
recent latency climbing towards the timeout.

## Not all requests are equal

When you must turn some work away, choose which. Snip ranks its traffic:

| Priority | Traffic | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | <Term t="load-balancer">Load balancer</Term> <Term t="health-check">health checks</Term> | Fail these and the load balancer removes the server, making everything worse. |
| 2 | Redirects | The core promise; every failed redirect is a broken link on someone else's site. |
| 3 | Creating links | Important, but a short delay is tolerable, and clients can retry with <Term t="idempotent">idempotency</Term> keys. |
| 4 | Dashboard and stats | Owners can wait a minute. |
| 5 | Bulk exports and reports | Can be paused entirely during a spike. |

As load rises, Snip sheds from the bottom of the table up.

## Don't work on requests nobody is waiting for

Two more habits keep goodput high:

- **Deadlines.** The client sends how long it will wait (or each hop passes on the time remaining). A server that
  picks up a request whose deadline has already passed drops it instead of doing it. A request halfway through that
  runs out of time stops early.
- **Graceful degradation.** Instead of failing outright, return something cheaper: a cached stats page from a minute
  ago, the dashboard without its live counter, the redirect without recording the click.

<Analogy>
  **A restaurant on a packed night.** A good host says "the wait is two hours" at the door, rather than seating everyone and letting the kitchen drown so that nobody's food arrives hot. Diners who already have a table are served first, takeaway orders are paused, and the dessert menu might be trimmed. Saying no at the door is what keeps the dining room working.
</Analogy>

<Mistakes>
  <ul>
    <li>**Unbounded queues.** They hide overload as latency, then run out of memory. Set limits and reject when full.</li>
    <li>**Shedding health checks.** Failing them makes the load balancer remove servers, concentrating load on fewer. Serve them first.</li>
    <li>**Treating all traffic the same.** Decide in advance what to drop first, so the core feature survives.</li>
    <li>**Finishing doomed requests.** If the client has given up, stop. Pass deadlines along each hop.</li>
  </ul>
</Mistakes>

## Recap

<Recap>
  <ul>
    <li>Past capacity, throughput can stay high while goodput (useful, on-time answers) collapses.</li>
    <li>Bound every queue; when full, push back upstream instead of growing.</li>
    <li>Shed load early and cheaply, lowest priority first; keep health checks and core requests.</li>
    <li>Pass deadlines along and drop requests nobody is waiting for; degrade gracefully where you can.</li>
  </ul>
</Recap>

## Interview questions

>
  <p>The service should keep serving as many requests as it can within their deadlines, and quickly reject the rest, rather than accepting everything and timing out on all of it. That means bounded queues, admission control based on in-flight requests or latency, and cheap 503s with Retry-After for rejected work. I'd shed by priority: health checks and core user-facing requests last, background and bulk work first, and degrade features where possible, like serving cached data.</p>
  <p>Deadlines should propagate so no hop works on requests the client has abandoned, clients should retry with backoff, jitter and a retry budget, and autoscaling adds capacity over the following minutes.</p>
