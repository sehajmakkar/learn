## One click, many steps

Someone in Berlin clicks `https://snip.link/x7Kp`. Snip's servers spend about 5 ms finding the long URL. Yet the
person waits several hundred milliseconds before anything happens. Almost all of that time is spent **setting up the
conversation**, not having it.

Four protocols take turns, each building on the one before:

| Step | Protocol | The question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **DNS** | Which IP address is `snip.link`? |
| 2 | **<Term t="tcp">TCP</Term>** | Can we open a reliable connection to that address? |
| 3 | **TLS** | Is this really Snip, and can we talk privately? |
| 4 | **HTTP** | Where does `/x7Kp` go? |

Count the round trips: one or more for DNS, one for TCP, one for TLS, one for the request. At 100 ms each that's
**400 ms or more** for 5 ms of real work, before the browser even starts on the destination site.

## The second click is much cheaper

Almost every step has a <Term t="cache">cache</Term> or a reusable result:

- **DNS answers are cached** by the browser, the operating system and the resolver, for as long as the <Term t="ttl">TTL</Term> allows.
- **Connections are kept open.** HTTP keep-alive lets the browser reuse the TCP and TLS connection for later
  requests, so it pays the handshakes once.
- **TLS sessions can resume**, skipping some of the work on reconnect.

So a returning visitor's click often costs a single round trip. That's why the first visit to a site feels slower
than the rest, and why benchmarks that only measure warm connections can mislead.

<Analogy>
  **Making a phone call to a business.** You look up the number (DNS), dial and wait for someone to pick up (TCP), confirm you're talking to the right company and agree to speak privately (TLS), and only then ask your question (HTTP). Calling the same person back later is quicker: you already have the number, and if they kept the line open, you just keep talking.
</Analogy>

## What this means for design

- **Round trips, not bandwidth, dominate small requests.** Snip's redirect is a few hundred bytes. Making it faster
  means fewer or shorter round trips: keep connections open, and put servers closer (<L id="02.05" />).
- **DNS TTLs decide how fast you can move.** If Snip's record has a TTL of a day and you move to new servers, some
  users keep going to the old address for up to a day. Teams lower the TTL ahead of a planned move, then raise it
  again afterwards.
- **DNS can send users to different places.** Many services answer the same name with different addresses depending
  on where the user is, or which servers are healthy. It's a coarse form of load balancing (<L id="03.06" />), limited
  by caching: a change only takes effect as caches expire.
- **DNS is part of your availability.** If nobody can look up your name, it doesn't matter that every server is up.
  That's why serious services use more than one DNS provider or name server location.

Newer HTTP versions cut the setup cost further. HTTP/2 sends many requests over one connection at once, and HTTP/3
runs over <Term t="quic">QUIC</Term>, which combines the transport and encryption handshakes into one round trip. That's
<L id="03.02" />.

<Mistakes>
  <ul>
    <li>**Ignoring setup cost.** A 5 ms handler behind three handshakes across an ocean is a 400 ms request. Measure from the user's side.</li>
    <li>**Long DNS TTLs before a move.** Lower them well ahead of time, or old caches keep sending traffic to old servers.</li>
    <li>**Treating DNS as someone else's problem.** If your name doesn't resolve, your whole service is down.</li>
  </ul>
</Mistakes>

## Recap

<Recap>
  <ul>
    <li>A first request costs a DNS lookup, a TCP handshake, a TLS handshake, then the HTTP request: several round trips.</li>
    <li>Caching DNS answers and reusing connections makes later requests far cheaper.</li>
    <li>DNS TTLs control how quickly traffic moves when you change an address.</li>
    <li>DNS is part of your availability; if names don't resolve, nothing works.</li>
  </ul>
</Recap>

  <p>The browser resolves the hostname to an IP address with DNS, checking its own cache and the operating system's, then asking a recursive resolver, which may walk from the root servers to the top-level domain's servers to the site's own name servers. It opens a TCP connection with a three-way handshake, then a TLS handshake to verify the certificate and agree on keys, one round trip in TLS 1.3.   <p>On later requests, DNS answers are cached for their TTL and the connection is kept alive, so most of the setup round trips disappear.</p>
