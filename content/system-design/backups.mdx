## The problem: a script deletes the wrong links

A Snip engineer runs a clean-up script meant to delete expired links for one customer. A bug in its filter deletes
2.3 million live links across 900 customers. The delete is committed, replicated to every replica in seconds, and
removed from the cache. Replication did its job perfectly: every copy now faithfully lacks the links.

**Replicas copy mistakes.** Only a copy taken **before** the mistake, kept where the mistake couldn't reach, can undo
it. That's what backups are for.

## Two numbers to decide first

- **RPO** (recovery point objective): how much recent data you can afford to lose. "No more than 5 minutes of
  changes."
- **RTO** (recovery time objective): how long you can afford to be without the data. "Restored within 4 hours."

They drive everything else. Nightly backups give an RPO of up to 24 hours. Restoring a 2 TB database from object
storage takes hours, which sets a floor on RTO.

## Point-in-time recovery

Postgres's approach (and most databases have an equivalent): take a **base backup**, a full copy of the data files,
regularly, and **archive every write-ahead log segment** as it fills (<L id="04.06" />). To recover, restore the base
backup and replay the archived log up to any moment you choose: 14:31:59, one second before the script ran.

That gives an RPO of minutes (how often log segments are archived) and lets you recover to just before a mistake,
not just to last night.

For Snip's accident, a full restore would also roll back every legitimate change since 14:32. So the usual move is to
**restore to a separate database** at 14:31:59 and copy just the deleted links back into production.

## Keep backups out of reach

Backups protect only if the thing that destroyed production can't destroy them too:

- **Separate account and credentials.** A compromised or mistaken production credential shouldn't be able to delete
  backups.
- **Immutable or versioned storage** with retention locks, so backups can't be deleted early, even by an admin.
- **Another region**, against losing a whole region (<L id="14.08" />).
- **Checksums** on every backup file, verified (<L id="09.06" />).

## Test the restore

A backup you've never restored is a hope, not a backup. Restore regularly into a scratch environment, automatically,
check row counts and a few known records, and time it: that measured time is your real RTO. Postgres's own
documentation tells you to set up and test WAL archiving before taking your first base backup.

Also test the **shape** of restore you'll need. Restoring a whole database is one thing; restoring 900 customers'
links from it, while the other customers keep working, is a different procedure, and it should exist before the day
you need it.

<Analogy>
  **A photocopy of your passport, kept at a friend's house.** Your phone's photo of it syncs to every device you own, so when you delete it by mistake, it vanishes from all of them (replication). The paper copy at a friend's house, taken last month, is still there. And you only know it's legible if you've actually looked at it.
</Analogy>

<Mistakes>
  <ul>
    <li>**Treating replicas as backups.** They copy mistakes within seconds.</li>
    <li>**Backups reachable with production credentials.** Separate accounts, immutable storage.</li>
    <li>**Never restoring.** Test restores regularly and measure the time.</li>
    <li>**Only whole-database restores.** Practise partial restores, like one customer's data, before you need them.</li>
  </ul>
</Mistakes>

## Recap

<Recap>
  <ul>
    <li>Replication protects against failures; backups protect against mistakes, bugs and attackers.</li>
    <li>Set RPO (data you can lose) and RTO (time to recover) first; they drive the design.</li>
    <li>Base backups plus archived WAL give point-in-time recovery to just before a mistake.</li>
    <li>Keep backups isolated and immutable, and test full and partial restores on a schedule.</li>
  </ul>
</Recap>

## Interview questions

<InterviewCard
  q={{ q: "How would you design backups for a production database? What are RPO and RTO?", level: "Mid", frequency: 2, importance: "Must-know", kind: "Concept" }}
  followUps={["Why aren't replicas enough?", "How do you recover from an accidental delete without losing other changes?"]}
>
  <p>RPO is how much data you can afford to lose, and RTO is how long recovery may take. For a relational database I'd take regular base backups and continuously archive the write-ahead log, enabling point-in-time recovery with an RPO of minutes. Backups go to immutable, versioned storage in a separate account and region, with checksums, so the same mistake or attacker can't remove them.</p>
  <p>Replicas aren't backups because they replicate deletes and corruption immediately. For an accidental delete I'd restore to a separate instance at a point just before it and copy back only the affected rows, so other changes aren't lost. And I'd restore automatically on a schedule to verify backups and measure the actual RTO, including partial restores for a single customer.</p>
</InterviewCard>
