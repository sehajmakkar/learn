## Five words that need precise meanings

People say "the system should be reliable and fast" and mean five different things. Separating them matters, because
they're bought in different ways and they pull against each other:

| Quality | The question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| **Availability** | Does the system answer when asked? |
| **Durability** | Once it says "saved", is the data still there later? |
| **Consistency** | Does everyone see the same, latest data? |
| **Latency** | How long does one request take? |
| **Cost** | What does it take, in money and effort, to run? |

## Availability

Availability is the share of time (or of requests) the system works. It's quoted in **nines**: 99.9% is "three
nines". Each extra nine cuts the downtime you're allowed by ten:

| Availability | Downtime per year | Per month | Per day |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 99% | 3.65 days | 7.2 hours | 14.4 minutes |
| 99.9% | 8.76 hours | 43.2 minutes | 1.44 minutes |
| 99.99% | 52.6 minutes | 4.32 minutes | 8.64 seconds |
| 99.999% | 5.26 minutes | 25.9 seconds | 0.87 seconds |

Figures from the availability table in Google's <a href="https://sre.google/sre-book/availability-table/">Site Reliability Engineering</a> book.

Two pieces of arithmetic come up again and again:

- **Parts in a chain multiply.** If a request needs the app *and* the database, and each is up 99.9% of the time,
  the request succeeds about 99.9% × 99.9% ≈ **99.8%** of the time. Every hop you add lowers availability.
- **Spare copies multiply the failure chance.** If either of two independent copies can serve a request, and each is
  down 1% of the time, both are down only 1% × 1% = 0.01% of the time: **99.99%** available. Redundancy is how you
  buy nines, as long as the copies don't fail together.

<Analogy>
  **Christmas lights.** In an old string wired in series, one dead bulb takes out the whole string: more bulbs, more ways to fail. Wire them in parallel and one dead bulb leaves the rest shining. A request path through many services is the series string; <Term t="replica">replicas</Term> behind a <Term t="load-balancer">load balancer</Term> are the parallel one.
</Analogy>

## Durability

Durability is the promise that data you've accepted won't be lost. It's different from availability: a system can be
down for an hour and lose nothing (low availability, high durability), or stay up while quietly losing writes (the
reverse, and much worse).

Durability is bought with copies in separate places: several disks, several machines, several buildings. Amazon S3
is designed for 99.999999999% ("eleven nines") durability of objects over a year, but 99.99% availability: the
data is almost never lost, though now and then you briefly can't reach it.

## Consistency

Consistency here means: after a write, do readers see it? Two broad answers:

- **Strong consistency:** every read sees the latest write. Simple to reason about, but every copy has to agree
  before you answer, which costs time and availability.
- **Eventual consistency:** copies catch up after a short delay, so a read may briefly return old data. Faster and
  more available, but your design has to tolerate stale reads.

Most systems mix them, feature by feature. Snip needs a new link to work the moment it's created (the person who made
it will click it straight away), but click counts can lag by a minute and nobody will notice.

## Latency and throughput

**Latency** is how long one request takes. **Throughput** is how many requests the system handles per second. They're
related but different: a highway can carry thousands of cars an hour (throughput) while each car still takes twenty
minutes to drive it (latency).

Never describe latency with an average. Suppose 98 of 100 requests take 20 ms and 2 take 2 seconds. The average is
about 60 ms, a number no request actually experienced. Use **percentiles** instead:

- **p50** (the median): half of requests are faster than this. Here, 20 ms.
- **p99**: 99% are faster than this. Here, 2 seconds. That's the slow tail, and at high traffic it's someone every
  second.

That's why Snip's requirement reads "under 50 ms at p99", not "fast on average". You'll see why the tail matters so
much in <L id="03.12" />.

## Cost

Cost is money (machines, storage, bandwidth, managed services) and also effort: every extra component is something
to deploy, monitor, upgrade and get paged for at 3 a.m. A design that meets every other requirement but needs a team of
ten to run it may still be the wrong design.

<Mistakes>
  <ul>
    <li>**Mixing up availability and durability.** "Our database is replicated, so it's highly available" might be true, or the replicas might all be in one building that loses power together.</li>
    <li>**Quoting average latency.** Averages hide the slow requests users complain about. Use p50 and p99.</li>
    <li>**Asking for five nines everywhere.** Each nine costs roughly ten times more effort. Spend it on the paths that need it.</li>
  </ul>
</Mistakes>

## Recap

<Recap>
  <ul>
    <li>Availability: does it answer? Measured in nines; chains multiply, redundancy helps.</li>
    <li>Durability: is saved data still there? Bought with copies in separate places.</li>
    <li>Consistency: do readers see the latest write? Strong or eventual, chosen per feature.</li>
    <li>Latency is per request and quoted as percentiles; throughput is requests per second.</li>
    <li>Cost includes the people who run it, not just the machines.</li>
  </ul>
</Recap>
