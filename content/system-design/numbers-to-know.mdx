## Why memorise any numbers?

You can't judge a design without a sense of scale. Is one more network hop a big deal? Should this lookup hit memory or
disk? Will five years of data fit on one machine? A few numbers, known to the nearest power of ten, answer most of
these questions in your head.

You don't need exact values. Hardware changes every year. What lasts are the **ratios**: memory is about a thousand
times faster than an <Term t="ssd">SSD</Term> read, and a round trip across the world is hundreds of times slower than one inside a
datacenter.

What to take away:

- **Memory is fast; everything else is far away.** Reading RAM takes about 100 ns. That's why <Term t="cache">caches</Term> (memory) sit in
  front of databases (disk), as you'll see in <L id="06.01" />.
- **A network hop inside a datacenter costs about half a millisecond.** A request that calls ten services one after
  another has spent 5 ms just travelling.
- **Crossing an ocean costs about 150 ms**, and the speed of light means no hardware upgrade fixes it. The only fix is
  to put data closer to users, which is what <Term t="cdn">CDNs</Term> and multi-region designs do.
- **Sequential beats random.** Reading data that sits together is far cheaper than jumping around. Databases and logs
  are built around this.

## Sizes

Data sizes go up in steps of about a thousand. Powers of two line up with them closely enough for estimates:

| Power of two | Roughly | Name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2¹⁰ | a thousand (10³) | kilobyte (KB) |
| 2²⁰ | a million (10⁶) | megabyte (MB) |
| 2³⁰ | a billion (10⁹) | gigabyte (GB) |
| 2⁴⁰ | a trillion (10¹²) | terabyte (TB) |
| 2⁵⁰ | a quadrillion (10¹⁵) | petabyte (PB) |

And the sizes of everyday things:

| Thing | Size |
| --- | --- |
| A character of English text | 1 byte |
| A 64-bit number or timestamp | 8 bytes |
| A <Term t="uuid">UUID</Term> | 16 bytes (36 as text) |
| A tweet-sized post with its metadata | about 1 KB |
| A compressed photo | a few hundred KB to a few MB |
| A minute of HD video | tens of MB |

## Time

Estimates usually turn "per day" into "per second". Two conversions do most of the work:

- **A day has 86,400 seconds.** Call it **100,000** (10⁵). Then a million events a day is about 10 a second.
- **A month has about 2.5 million seconds**, and a year about 30 million.

Rounding a day up to 100,000 seconds makes your per-second numbers about 15% low. That's fine for an estimate, and you
can say so.

<Mistakes>
  <ul>
    <li>**Memorising digits instead of ratios.** Whether an SSD read is 16 µs or 100 µs matters less than knowing it's about a thousand times slower than memory.</li>
    <li>**Ignoring the network.** A design that makes ten calls in a row for one user request pays for ten round trips before it does any work.</li>
    <li>**Forgetting physics.** No amount of servers makes a single round trip between continents faster than about 100–150 ms.</li>
  </ul>
</Mistakes>

## Recap

<Recap>
  <ul>
    <li>Memory ~100 ns, SSD read ~16 µs, datacenter round trip ~0.5 ms, intercontinental ~150 ms.</li>
    <li>Sizes step by a thousand: KB, MB, GB, TB, PB.</li>
    <li>A day is about 10⁵ seconds: a million a day is about 10 a second.</li>
    <li>Remember the ratios; the exact values change with hardware.</li>
  </ul>

## Interview questions

  q={{ q: "Why put a cache in front of a database? Answer with rough numbers.", level: "Junior", frequency: 3, importance: "Must-know", kind: "Concept" }}

>
  <p>A cache keeps hot data in memory. Reading memory takes about 100 ns, while a database query usually involves a network round trip (about 0.5 ms inside a datacenter) plus disk or index work, so a few milliseconds in total. Serving a popular item from a cache can be ten times faster or more, and it takes that load off the database.</p>
  <p>The cost is a second copy of the data that can go stale, extra memory, and another component to run.</p>
