## The problem: 2 PB of archives, kept safe, cheaply

Snip's archived click history (<L id="09.04" />) has grown to 2 PB. Disks fail all the time at that scale: with tens
of thousands of drives, several die every week. The archive must survive that, and survive losing a whole rack or
data centre. Keeping three full copies would mean buying 6 PB of disk to store 2 PB.

## Replication: whole copies

Store each piece of data on three machines, in different racks or zones. Lose any two and the third still has
everything.

- **Overhead**: 200% (3 bytes stored per byte of data).
- **Reads**: any one copy answers, so reads are simple and fast.
- **Repair**: copy the data from a surviving replica. Cheap and quick.

That's ideal for **hot** data: frequently read, latency-sensitive, small.

## Erasure coding: pieces plus parity

Split the data into **k** data pieces, compute **m** parity pieces from them, and store all **k + m** on different
machines. The code is designed so that **any k of the k + m pieces** rebuild the original. Lose up to m pieces and
nothing is lost.

The simplest example is one parity piece made by XOR, as in RAID 5:

```ts
export function parity(shards: Buffer[]): Buffer {
  const p = Buffer.alloc(shards[0].length);
  for (const s of shards) for (let i = 0; i < p.length; i++) p[i] ^= s[i];
  return p;
}

/** Any one missing shard is the XOR of all the others, parity included. */
export const rebuildMissing = (survivors: Buffer[]) => parity(survivors);
```

Tested with three 8-byte shards ("Snip cli", "ck histo", "ry 2026!"): XOR of the parity and two survivors rebuilt the
missing middle shard exactly. XOR tolerates one loss; **Reed-Solomon** codes generalise it to any m.

| Scheme | Pieces | Survives losing | Storage per byte of data |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3× replication | 3 copies | 2 | 3.0× |
| RS(10, 4) | 10 data + 4 parity | 4 | 1.4× |
| RS(17, 3) | 17 data + 3 parity | 3 | about 1.18× |
| XOR (RAID 5 style), 4 + 1 | 4 data + 1 parity | 1 | 1.25× |

RS(10, 4) survives **more** failures than triple replication while storing **less than half** as many bytes. For 2 PB
of archive, that's 2.8 PB of disk instead of 6.

## What erasure coding costs

- **Repair is expensive.** Rebuilding one lost piece means reading k others across the network, and computing. With
  RS(10, 4), losing one 1 GB piece costs 10 GB of reads. Replication's repair reads 1 GB.
- **Reads can be slower.** A whole-object read gathers k pieces from k machines; if one is slow or gone, the reader
  must fetch a parity piece and decode (<L id="03.12" />).
- **Small objects don't split well.** Splitting a 5 KB object into 14 pieces is silly, so systems erasure-code large
  stripes or volumes made of many objects (<L id="09.04" />).
- **Placement matters.** All k + m pieces must be on independent failure domains, or one rack failure takes out
  several.

## Hot, warm, cold

Most large storage systems use both: replicate hot data, and erasure-code data once it cools down. Facebook moved
older, less-requested photos and videos from Haystack (replicated) to f4, an erasure-coded system for "warm"
data, cutting the effective copies stored per byte from 3.6 to 2.8, or 2.1 with a cross-datacenter scheme.

Snip does the same with click history: the current month lives in the replicated LSM cluster (<L id="09.03" />),
three copies; monthly archives go to object storage, which erasure-codes them.

<Analogy>
  **Photocopies versus a Sudoku.** Three photocopies of a document survive two being lost, but cost three times the paper. Erasure coding is more like a puzzle with built-in redundancy: tear out some squares and the rest are enough to work out what was there, as long as not too many are missing. It's far less paper, but filling in a missing square takes work.
</Analogy>

<Mistakes>
  <ul>
    <li>**Erasure-coding hot, small, latency-sensitive data.** Decoding and fan-out reads cost latency. Replicate it.</li>
    <li>**Ignoring repair traffic.** Rebuilding pieces reads k times as much over the network; plan for it after a failure.</li>
    <li>**Pieces sharing a failure domain.** Spread pieces across racks and zones, or one failure removes several.</li>
    <li>**Confusing redundancy with backup.** Neither protects against a bad delete or corruption written to every copy.</li>
  </ul>
</Mistakes>

## Recap

<Recap>
  <ul>
    <li>Replication keeps whole copies: simple, fast reads and repair, but 3× storage.</li>
    <li>Erasure coding stores k data plus m parity pieces; any k rebuild the data, for far less storage.</li>
    <li>It costs expensive repairs, slower reads when a piece is missing, and doesn't suit small objects.</li>
    <li>Replicate hot data; erasure-code warm and cold data across independent failure domains.</li>
  </ul>
</Recap>

  <p>Erasure coding splits data into k pieces and computes m parity pieces so that any k of the k + m rebuild the original.
