## The problem: "redirects are slow" at 14:07

An owner reports that Snip links "hang for a second" since lunchtime. Snip has 40 app servers, Redis, Postgres shards,
a CDN and a dozen services. Somewhere among them, something changed. The team needs to answer three questions, fast:
**is something wrong** (and for whom), **where**, and **why**.

Each question is best answered by a different kind of telemetry.

## Metrics: is something wrong?

**Metrics** are numbers over time: requests per second, error rate, p99 latency, queue depth, CPU. They're cheap to
store (one number per series per interval, however much traffic there is), fast to query over months, and what alerts
are built on.

Google's SRE book names the **four golden signals** to measure for any user-facing service:

- **Latency**: how long requests take. Track successful and failed requests separately: a fast error and a slow
  success mean different things.
- **Traffic**: demand, in the service's own terms (redirects per second).
- **Errors**: the rate of failing requests, whether explicit (500s), implicit (wrong content) or by policy (too slow).
- **Saturation**: how "full" the service is: the resource closest to its limit.

The cost to watch is **cardinality**: every distinct combination of labels is a separate series. `latency{route,
status}` is fine; `latency{link_code}` across 600 million links is 600 million series and will sink the metrics
system. Per-link numbers belong in the analytics pipeline (<L id="11.01" />), not in operational metrics.

## Logs: what happened, exactly?

**Logs** are records of individual events: this request, this error, this retry. They have all the detail metrics
throw away, so they answer "which requests?" and "what did the error say?".

- **Structured logs** (JSON with fields such as `route`, `status`, `duration_ms`, `trace_id`) can be filtered and
  counted; free text can only be searched.
- **Volume is the cost.** At 100,000 redirects a second, a log line per redirect is billions a day. Log every error
  and every slow request, and sample the rest.
- **Never log secrets or personal data** you don't need: passwords, tokens, full <Term t="signed-url">signed URLs</Term> (<L id="13.05" />).

## Traces: where did the time go?

A request for `x7Kp` passes through the load balancer, an app server, Redis, maybe a Postgres shard. **Distributed
tracing** follows it: each step is a **span** with a start time, a duration and attributes, and spans link into a tree
by passing a **trace ID** (and the parent span's ID) along with every call. Google's Dapper paper, the ancestor of most
tracing systems, describes exactly this: a trace tree of spans, with context propagated through common libraries.

Tracing everything is expensive, so traces are **sampled**. Dapper found that even sampling 1 in 1,024 requests still
gave enough data for high-volume services. Many systems also keep every trace that errored or was unusually slow
(tail-based sampling), since those are the ones you'll want.

Today, **OpenTelemetry** is the common standard for producing all three signals, with spans, attributes and context
propagation defined the same way across languages and vendors.

## How they fit together during an incident

1. A **metric** alert fires: p99 redirect latency over 200 ms for 5 minutes.
2. The dashboard narrows it: only redirects that miss the cache; only on shard 7.
3. An exemplar **trace** from the slow requests shows 900 ms in one Postgres query.
4. That trace's ID finds the **logs**: the query plan changed after a deploy at 13:58 dropped an index.

Linking them is what makes them powerful: every log line and metric exemplar carries the trace ID.

<Analogy>
  **A hospital patient.** The monitor beside the bed shows vital signs continuously (metrics), and beeps when one goes out of range. The chart records each event in detail: doses, symptoms, times (logs). And the patient's journey through admissions, scans and wards, with how long each took, is the trace. A nurse starts from the monitor, the doctor reads the chart, and the trace shows where the delay was.
</Analogy>

<Mistakes>
  <ul>
    <li>**High-cardinality metric labels.** User IDs, link codes and URLs belong in logs and traces, not metric labels.</li>
    <li>**Averages for latency.** Use percentiles, and separate successes from failures.</li>
    <li>**Unstructured logs.** Use fields, including a trace ID, so logs can be filtered and joined.</li>
    <li>**Alerting on causes.** Page on symptoms users feel (errors, latency); use causes for diagnosis.</li>
  </ul>
</Mistakes>

## Recap

<Recap>
  <ul>
    <li>Metrics say whether something is wrong; logs say what happened; traces say where the time went.</li>
    <li>Measure the four golden signals: latency, traffic, errors, saturation.</li>
    <li>Watch cardinality in metrics and volume in logs; sample traces but keep the slow and failed ones.</li>
    <li>Link everything with a trace ID so an alert leads to a trace and then to logs.</li>
  </ul>
</Recap>
