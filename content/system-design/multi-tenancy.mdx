## The problem: one agency slows down everyone

Snip's 40,000 paying customers share the same servers, queues and databases. One agency launches a campaign that sends
a burst of 2 million link creations through the <Term t="api">API</Term>, plus an export of three years of click history. For an hour, every
other customer's link creation is slow and their exports sit queued. Nobody broke a rule; one tenant simply used a lot.

That's the **noisy neighbour** problem, and every shared (multi-tenant) system has it.

## How much to share

| Model | What tenants share | Isolation | Cost per tenant |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pooled** | Everything: servers, tables (with a tenant ID column), queues | Lowest | Lowest |
| **Partitioned** | Infrastructure, but each tenant's data in its own schema, shard or queue | Medium | Medium |
| **Dedicated (silo)** | Nothing: a separate stack per tenant | Highest | Highest |

Most products mix them: pooled for the long tail, dedicated for the largest customers or those with strict compliance
needs. Snip pools everyone, gives its top 20 agencies their own database shards and search indexes
(<L id="10.04" />), and offers a dedicated deployment on its enterprise plan.

## Limits per tenant

The first defence is making sure no tenant can take more than its share:

- **<Term t="rate-limit">Rate limits</Term> per tenant** on every expensive operation (<L id="14.03" />), set by plan.
- **Concurrency limits per tenant**: at most 5 exports running at once per customer, however many they request.
- **Fair queuing**: instead of one first-in-first-out queue, round-robin between tenants' queues, so one tenant's 10,000
  queued exports don't sit in front of everyone else's one.
- **Quotas on size**: links per account, storage per account, so growth is a conversation, not an incident.

## Cells: cap the blast radius

Limits stop one tenant from using too much; they don't help if a tenant triggers a bug that crashes the servers they
run on. **Cells** split the system into several complete, independent copies, each serving a subset of tenants. A
poison request or a bad deploy (<L id="14.05" />) can only take down one cell: a fraction of customers instead of all of
them. Cells also cap how big any one deployment gets, so each is tested at a known scale.

## Shuffle sharding

Cells divide tenants into fixed groups, so each tenant shares a fate with everyone in its cell. **Shuffle sharding**
does better. Give each tenant a random **combination** of a few workers out of many. Two tenants rarely get exactly the
same combination, so a tenant that poisons its workers takes out only its own combination; other tenants usually
share at most one of those workers, and still have the rest of theirs.

Amazon's numbers: with 8 workers split into 4 regular shards of 2, a bad tenant affects 25% of tenants. With shuffle
shards of 2 out of the same 8 workers, there are 28 combinations, and the impact drops to about 1/28th. Route 53 uses
2,048 virtual name servers and gives each domain a shuffle shard of 4, which allows about 730 billion combinations.

<Analogy>
  **An apartment block.** Shared plumbing is cheap, but one flat running every tap starves the others of water pressure (noisy neighbour), so each flat gets a flow limit. The building is split into separate wings with their own pipes (cells), so a burst pipe floods one wing, not all. And if each flat drew from a random pair of several water tanks, one contaminated tank would affect only the few flats that drew on it, and most of them still have their other tank.
</Analogy>

<Mistakes>
  <ul>
    <li>**One shared FIFO queue for all tenants.** One tenant's backlog delays everyone. Fair-queue by tenant.</li>
    <li>**Limits only on request rate.** Also cap concurrency, size and storage per tenant.</li>
    <li>**One giant deployment.** Cells and shuffle sharding cap the blast radius of bad tenants and bad deploys.</li>
    <li>**The same tenancy for everyone.** Give the largest or most sensitive customers dedicated resources.</li>
  </ul>
</Mistakes>

## Recap

<Recap>
  <ul>
    <li>Multi-tenancy trades cost for isolation: pooled, partitioned, or dedicated per tenant.</li>
    <li>Rate limits, concurrency limits, fair queuing and quotas keep each tenant to its share.</li>
    <li>Cells limit how many tenants a failure can reach.</li>
    <li>Shuffle sharding gives each tenant a random set of resources, so one tenant's bad day touches almost nobody else.</li>
  </ul>
</Recap>

>
  <p>Start with per-tenant limits on rate, concurrency, size and storage, set by plan and enforced at the gateway and in each service, and replace shared FIFO queues with per-tenant or fair-share queues so one tenant's backlog doesn't block others. Monitor usage per tenant to find noisy ones early.</p>
  <p>To limit blast radius from bugs or poison requests, split the system into cells, each a full stack serving a subset of tenants, and consider shuffle sharding, where each tenant gets a random combination of workers so two tenants rarely share all their resources. Large or regulated customers can get dedicated shards or stacks, at a higher price.</p>
