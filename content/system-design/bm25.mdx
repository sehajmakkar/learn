## The problem: 4,000 matches, which first?

The agency searches `summer shoes` and 4,000 of its links match. Nobody reads past the first ten. Which ten? Sorting
by date ignores relevance; sorting by "contains both words" doesn't separate them. A search engine needs a **score**
for how well each document matches the query. Three ideas build it.

## 1. Term frequency: more mentions, more relevant

A title that says "shoes" twice is probably more about shoes than one that says it once. So count each query term's
occurrences in the document: its **term frequency** (TF).

The flaw shows straight away: a spammy title that repeats "shoes" five times beats a title that's exactly what the
user wants.

## 2. Inverse document frequency: rare words matter more

In the agency's links, "sale" appears in half of all titles, and "sandals" in very few. Matching "sandals" says much
more about a document than matching "sale". **Inverse document frequency** (IDF) weights each term by how rare it is,
typically the logarithm of (number of documents ÷ documents containing the term). **TF-IDF** multiplies the two.

## 3. BM25: saturation and length

**BM25**, the default ranking function in Lucene, Elasticsearch and OpenSearch, keeps IDF and fixes TF in two ways:

- **Saturation.** Each extra occurrence adds less, and a term's contribution levels off at a ceiling, however often it
  repeats. Robertson and Zaragoza's review of the framework behind BM25 calls this saturation: one term's
  contribution can't exceed a limit, however frequently it occurs. The parameter **k1** controls how fast it
  saturates (default 1.2).
- **Length normalization.** A long document mentions more words just by being long. BM25 compares each document's
  length with the average and discounts term counts in longer ones. The parameter **b** controls how strongly (0 = not
  at all, 1 = fully; default 0.75).

For each query term, BM25 adds:

```
idf(term) × tf × (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 × (1 − b + b × length / averageLength))
```

What the lab shows for `summer shoes`:

| Scoring | First | Second |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Count matches | "Shoes, shoes, shoes…" (5) | "Summer sale: shoes and sandals…" (2) |
| TF-IDF | "Shoes, shoes, shoes…" (3.47) | "Summer sale: shoes and sandals…" (1.61) |
| BM25 (k1 1.2, b 0.75) | **"Summer sale: shoes and sandals…" (1.52)** | "Shoes, shoes, shoes…" (1.04) |

Counting and TF-IDF both reward repetition. BM25 saturates the five "shoes" and penalises that title for being more
than twice the average length, so the title that matches **both** words wins. Set k1 to 0 and term frequency stops
counting at all: every match is worth only its IDF. Search `sale` with b at 0.75 and "Sale ends tonight", the shortest
title, comes first; set b to 0 and all four matches tie.

## Beyond BM25

BM25 scores text against text. Real ranking usually combines it with other signals:

- **Field weights**: a match in the title matters more than one in the notes.
- **Business signals**: recency, popularity (Snip boosts links with more clicks), the user's own links first.
- **Learning to rank**: a model trained on what people clicked reorders the top results BM25 found.
- **Meaning**: BM25 matches words, not ideas; "sneakers" won't find "running shoes". Vector search does
  (<L id="10.05" />).

<Analogy>
  **Judging a CV for a job.** Mentioning "Python" once means something; mentioning it twenty times doesn't make someone twenty times better (saturation). A rare skill like "Fortran" tells you more than "Microsoft Word" (IDF). And a forty-page CV will mention everything somewhere, so you allow for its length (normalization).
</Analogy>

<Mistakes>
  <ul>
    <li>**Ranking by match count.** Repetition wins. Use BM25.</li>
    <li>**One field for everything.** Titles, tags and body text deserve different weights.</li>
    <li>**Tuning k1 and b by feel.** Measure with real queries and judgments before and after.</li>
    <li>**Relevance only.** Users also want recent, popular and their own results; blend business signals in.</li>

## Recap

<Recap>
  <ul>
    <li>Term frequency rewards documents that mention query terms; IDF weights rare terms more.</li>
    <li>BM25 adds saturation (k1) so repeats stop counting, and length normalization (b) so long documents don't win by size.</li>
    <li>It's the default in Lucene-based engines, with k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75.</li>
    <li>Real ranking combines BM25 with field weights, business signals and sometimes learned models.</li>
  </ul>
</Recap>

  <p>TF-IDF scores a document by how often each query term appears in it, term frequency, multiplied by how rare the term is across the collection, inverse document frequency, so rare, specific terms count more than common ones. Its weakness is that raw term frequency rewards repetition and long documents.</p>
  <p>BM25 fixes both: term frequency saturates, so extra occurrences add less and less, controlled by k1, and term frequency is normalized by the document's length relative to the average, controlled by b. It's the default in Lucene, Elasticsearch and OpenSearch with k1 of 1.2 and b of 0.75. For a product search I'd add field boosts, such as title over description, plus signals like popularity, stock and recency, possibly with a learning-to-rank model reordering the top results.</p>
